# Kitchen materials strategy

**Status:** working design direction, not final specifications  
**Updated:** September 19, 2026

This file records the current material logic so individual selections can be judged against a coherent palette rather than chosen one at a time.

The key idea is **functional differentiation without material-showroom chaos**. Different surfaces may be appropriate because the perimeter, chef island and guest perch perform genuinely different jobs. Three materials are acceptable only if each has a clear role and the palette remains visually coherent.

## Current working hypothesis

| Zone | Current direction | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Perimeter counters | Light / medium-light quartzite or another quiet natural stone with real contrast | Hard-wearing architectural surface; visually lifts the room; can continue into selected backsplash areas |
| Chef island | Dark architectural soapstone, honed | Exceptional heat / acid / stain tolerance; tactile; appropriate for a real working surface; accepts patina |
| Guest perch | Furniture-quality wood top | Makes the perch read as furniture rather than Island #2; warmer social surface; gives the room another texture without another veined stone |

This is a hypothesis, not a specification. Cabinet wood, floor tone, light and actual slab samples may change it.

## Perimeter stone

Quartzite remains a strong candidate for the perimeter because it combines natural-stone character with high abrasion resistance. The desired slab should be relatively quiet but not disappear into an all-cream room.

The goal is **background with life**, not a dramatic feature slab that competes with every other surface.

A full-height slab backsplash can be useful behind cooking because it removes grout and is easy to wipe, but quartzite is not required simply for cleanability. Use it there when carrying the same stone vertically improves the architecture. Do not spend for quartzite on a backsplash merely because it is "the durable option."

Selection criteria:

- restrained movement rather than loud bookmatched drama;
- enough tonal range to create contrast with the cabinetry;
- acceptable porosity and sealing requirements for the exact slab;
- no dependence on a glossy finish to look finished;
- visually compatible with a dark soapstone work island.

## Chef island — soapstone

Soapstone is a particularly plausible material for the chef island because its weaknesses are mainly cosmetic while its strengths are operational.

### Why it fits

- Nonporous / highly stain-resistant in ordinary kitchen use.
- Excellent resistance to acids.
- Excellent heat tolerance.
- Honed, tactile surface rather than glossy "luxury stone" behavior.
- Fine scratches and wear can become patina rather than catastrophic damage.
- Many scratches can be visually blended or sanded locally.

### The real tradeoff

Soapstone **will scratch**. The decision is therefore psychological more than functional.

A working island should be expected to acquire fine rub marks, scratches and occasional small dings from cast iron, unglazed ceramic, grit and tools. If those marks will be read as failure, do not use soapstone. If they will be read as evidence of a working surface, soapstone is unusually well matched to the project.

Prefer a harder architectural soapstone rather than a particularly talc-rich soft variety.

### Before specifying

Obtain a meaningful sample of the actual soapstone under consideration and abuse it deliberately:

1. Leave part untreated and oil / wax part according to the fabricator's proposed finish.
2. Drag a ceramic plate across it.
3. Slide a cast-iron pan several inches.
4. Put down a stainless pan carelessly.
5. Make a deliberate shallow scratch.
6. Leave wine, oil, lemon and tomato on it for several hours / overnight.
7. Try the recommended scratch-blending procedure.

The liquids are expected to be boring. The scratch response is the decision test.

### Finish

Current preference: honed, low-sheen, allowed to age naturally. Oiling / waxing should be chosen for appearance and color consistency, not treated as a required stain-proofing system.

## Guest perch — wood

A wood top is attractive precisely because the perch should feel like furniture.

The required behavior is stricter than for a protected dining table: guests should be able to sit down with wine and food **without placemats or protective choreography**.

### Performance standard

A guest should be able to put a dinner plate, appetizer plate, wineglass or wet water glass directly on the surface and never think about protecting it.

Expected long-term wear:

- fine scratches: acceptable;
- minor dents: acceptable;
- gradual patina: acceptable;
- routine wine / water / oil staining: not acceptable;
- hot cast iron directly on the top: not acceptable; use a trivet.

### Finish strategy

For this specific use, favor a high-performance matte / satin film finish over a traditional oil or hard-wax finish.

Strong candidates to discuss with the finisher:

- sprayed 2K polyurethane;
- high-quality catalyzed conversion varnish or equivalent professional furniture coating.

The objective is a finish that still looks like wood, not a thick glossy bar-top coating.

Hard-wax oils such as Rubio / Osmo are attractive and repairable, but they are not the current first choice for a surface expected to receive unattended wine, condensation, sauce and oily food without placemats.

### Construction / appearance

Avoid:

- narrow-strip commercial butcher block;
- end-grain checkerboard;
- live-edge slab styling;
- exaggerated rustic figure;
- a thick "farmhouse" top that fights the cabinetry.

Prefer a refined furniture top made from a small number of well-matched boards, with restrained edge detailing.

A finished thickness around 1.5–1.75 inches is a useful starting point, subject to the final support design.

### Wood species

Not selected.

Current candidates should have calm grain and work with the eventual cabinet species. Straight-grained walnut is especially attractive if the cabinetry remains lighter. White oak is technically excellent but may be too repetitive if used extensively elsewhere. Sapele / mahogany-family options may be worth considering if a warmer furniture tone is useful.

The species should be selected *after* the cabinet wood is narrowed down.

## Cabinetry

Current direction:

- wood cabinetry;
- quiet, consistent, relatively straight grain;
- inset flat-panel doors with a subtle bead and enough frame substance to feel architectural;
- clean but not anonymous, and specifically not ultra-thin / skinny Shaker;
- functional reserved handles;
- limited upper cabinetry in the main kitchen.

Rift-sawn white oak is a reference point for grain quality, not yet a specification. If it remains the lead, test it in a warmer and somewhat richer light-medium / medium treatment rather than the pale raw-oak look that currently dates many white-oak kitchens. The nearby rift-sawn white-oak family-room shelves establish a positive grain reference but should not force a match.

Avoid highly figured wood that turns every door into an object. The cabinetry should establish a calm field against which the stone, metal and furniture-like perch can register.

See [VISUAL_REFERENCES.md](VISUAL_REFERENCES.md): the cabinet reference is useful for profile and proportion but intentionally rejected as an overall all-beige palette.

## Coherence rules

1. Do not choose each surface as an independent "favorite."
2. One material should not have to be the star from every angle.
3. If the perimeter stone has strong movement, simplify the other materials.
4. If the guest perch uses a distinctive wood, keep its form disciplined.
5. Dark soapstone works best when it creates deliberate contrast, not when every other surface is also dark.
6. The room should have enough contrast to avoid the all-beige luxury-kitchen effect.
7. Repetition should be selective: the same material can appear in more than one zone when it helps the room cohere, but duplication is not mandatory.

## Current palette question

The most promising current composition is:

**quiet lighter stone perimeter + dark soapstone chef island + furniture-grade wood guest perch + calm, somewhat richer wood cabinetry**

This combination is also a plausible way to achieve the newly preferred "a bit richer / more contrast" direction without adding decorative noise. The darker work island can provide mineral depth; the wood perch can provide warmth and furniture character.

The unresolved issue is the exact relationship between cabinet wood and perch wood. Solve those together, then select actual stone slabs against the wood samples rather than from photographs. Do not infer a dark stone top for the guest perch merely because one visual reference shows it; the current material hypothesis for the perch remains furniture-grade wood.

# Kitchen design intent

**Status:** current design-intent layer  
**Updated:** September 19, 2026

This file records the durable *why* behind the kitchen. Geometry, dimensions and tested clearances remain controlled by [CURRENT_STATE.json](CURRENT_STATE.json) and the active study linked from [START_HERE.md](../START_HERE.md). If a design move improves a drawing but violates the principles below, the drawing should lose.

## True north

The kitchen should be a serious working kitchen that also makes the house better to live in and better to host in. It is not a show kitchen, and it should not optimize occasional entertaining at the expense of daily cooking.

The backyard view and the breakfast-table area are core infrastructure, not leftover space. The kitchen table remains the primary everyday eating place and should continue to feel connected to the backyard. Future window/opening work should improve that relationship while addressing the current problem of strong south light and frequently lowered shades.

The remodel should feel refined and architectural without becoming precious. Materials may age. Surfaces may show honest use. The goal is not photographic perfection; it is a kitchen that remains attractive while being used hard.

## How the kitchen is actually used

- One primary cook does most of the serious cooking. The chef island is the principal prep station.
- The island prep sink stays. Intake, washing, cutting and offloading need a coherent working sequence.
- A second person should have a genuinely useful helper position rather than occupying the chef's primary work path.
- Daily cleanup and cooking outrank occasional hosting needs.
- Buffet staging is a separate function from the guest perch. Guests should not have to vacate their seats so food can be staged.
- Four guest places are useful for hosting, but the kitchen should not be permanently organized around four occupied stools. Everyday use should remain visually and physically lighter.
- Guests at the perch are typically drinking wine and eating small plates or appetizers, not treating it as a second formal dining table.
- The main table remains the normal everyday dining surface. The formal dining room remains available for more formal company.

## Spatial hierarchy

### 1. Chef island

The chef island is a **workbench first**. It should feel highly capable, compact and intentional rather than oversized for visual effect. The current working footprint is 36 × 66 inches; see the controlling geometry for exact placement and status.

The island should support prep, the prep sink, garbage/compost choreography and rapid movement to the cooking wall. Its surface material should tolerate heat, acid, oil, water and aggressive daily use without requiring protective rituals.

### 2. Perimeter / cooking wall

The perimeter should provide calm architectural continuity, practical landing zones and an easy-to-clean cooking environment. Minimize upper cabinets in the main kitchen where possible so the room reads as open and composed rather than storage-heavy.

Range-side mise matters more than symmetry. The limited counter around the existing range is a known problem; future cabinetry should protect useful landing and staging space near the cooktop.

### 3. Guest perch

The guest perch should **read as furniture, not Island #2**.

Its current study size is 72 × 42 inches. It should provide two comfortable everyday places and occasional additional places without becoming a giant permanent seating bar. The form, base and materials should reinforce that this is a social object adjacent to the kitchen rather than another block of kitchen cabinetry.

No waterfall stone treatment. Avoid the visual language of a second monolithic island.

### 4. Breakfast table / backyard edge

Preserve the breakfast table as the primary daily eating place. It should remain a focal point with the strongest possible relationship to the backyard.

The current horizontal windows, intervening columns and solar glare limit that relationship. Any architectural work in this zone should be judged partly by whether it creates a more expansive, usable view without producing intolerable glare.

### 5. Reclaimed old porch

The old screened porch is a **breakfast / beverage / coffee service zone**, not another eating area.

Its job is to take recurring traffic and countertop clutter out of the main kitchen: coffee, water, ice, breakfast equipment and potentially beverage refrigeration / cleanup. It should reduce trips through the chef's work zone and main refrigerator.

Do not turn it into a redundant dining destination simply because square footage becomes available.

## Hosting principles

Hospitality should feel easy, not staged.

The kitchen needs to support guests being present without making the cook perform around them. The perch should give guests a natural place to sit with wine and small plates while remaining outside the chef's primary movement. The helper should be able to contribute without becoming an obstacle.

For larger meals, buffet service is preferred. A successful plan provides a staging surface that can remain useful while the perch stays occupied.

Occasional hosting constraints can justify temporary stools, moving a chair or modest choreography. They should not dictate the entire everyday room.

## Visual language

The desired room is **reserved, warm and materially rich**, not beige-on-beige minimalism. The latest visual exploration clarified the target further: **a bit richer / more contrast wins**. Restraint should come from disciplined materials and proportions, not from washing the whole room in cream.

- Wood cabinetry with quiet, consistent, relatively straight grain.
- The preferred door language is inset, flat-panel cabinetry with a subtle bead and enough rail/stile substance to feel architectural.
- Avoid anonymous slab-door minimalism and ultra-thin / skinny Shaker proportions that feel fashion-driven.
- Functional, restrained hardware rather than decorative jewelry. Exact form and finish remain open.
- Minimize visual noise and unnecessary material changes, but allow deliberate tonal contrast.
- Stone should be selected for character and usefulness, not simply because it is expensive or photogenic.
- The cooking wall should stay quiet. The desired reaction is "beautiful cabinetry," not "wow, range wall."
- A likely 36-inch induction cooktop should not become an excuse for a heroic hood, faux hearth, Provence gesture or restaurant-kitchen theater.
- Stainless appliance faces are acceptable when they make functional and architectural sense. Panel-ready is a compositional option, not a premium-kitchen requirement.
- The guest perch is a deliberate opportunity for furniture-level detailing.

Rift-sawn white oak is admired for its calm grain, but it should not become an automatic default simply because it is currently fashionable. If oak wins, test a warmer, somewhat richer light-medium / medium finish rather than the pale raw-oak formula; avoid gray, orange and golden-oak effects. The nearby rift-sawn white-oak family-room shelves are a useful grain reference, not a mandate to match them.

## Decision hierarchy

When tradeoffs arise, use this order:

1. Daily cooking workflow and cleanup.
2. Backyard / breakfast-table relationship.
3. Clear circulation and a useful secondary helper position.
4. Hospitality without compromising the chef's core.
5. Durable, low-anxiety materials.
6. Visual coherence and architectural character.
7. Occasional maximum-capacity seating.
8. Novelty, trend or showroom impact.

A proposal that performs well on the first six and gives up a little on #7 is likely a better kitchen than one designed around peak guest count.

## What this kitchen should not become

It should not become a giant open-plan showroom with two indistinguishable stone islands, a wall of upper cabinetry and a collection of expensive appliances looking for jobs.

It should also not become so precious that ordinary cooking requires trivets, placemats, coasters and apologies.

The standard is a beautiful room whose materials and layout become better understood through use.

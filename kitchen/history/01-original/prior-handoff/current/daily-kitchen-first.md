## Next design pass: establish the daily baseline before fitting the guest surface

1. Account for the full existing bent cleanup run as the starting benchmark, within the real fixed wall. Establish usable dirty-dish landing/scraping, sink access, dishwasher loading on the right when facing the sink, clean drying/unloading space, and access to dish storage. Identify which surfaces can genuinely share duties and which must coexist during everyday use. Preserve or improve this function; retaining every existing bend is not mandatory, but deleting its usable capacity without replacement is unacceptable.
2. Fit the chef sequence alongside that cleanup program: island prep sink, 24 × 18 cutting board, intake and offload, washed produce/compost beyond the board, and a useful left-of-range mise landing that also accounts for hot pans. Test helper activity and open dishwasher against actual daily circulation. The 36 × 60 island is a study, not a fixed requirement.
3. Test separate guest seating against the daily baseline. Four remains requested; three remains an invited comparison. Change guest footprint, placement, connection or end shape before cutting essential cleanup capacity. Do not treat any guest configuration as fitting until the stair arrival and occupied chairs work.
4. Add the occasional buffet as an entertaining overlay. It must be possible to set it up while guests remain at the perch. The lower adjoining run, integrated porch transition, and well-designed temporary/drop-down surface remain candidates. The lower run only qualifies if its daily duties can actually be released at that moment; do not make the occupied perch or active cleanup landing disappear. Trace plate filling and travel to formal dining, with the less-friendly hall route retained as a fallback.

The next comparison should show where daily cleanup improves or loses capacity alongside every guest-seat gain. A proposal with missing everyday functions must not be ranked as the preferred plan just because its guest geometry is appealing.

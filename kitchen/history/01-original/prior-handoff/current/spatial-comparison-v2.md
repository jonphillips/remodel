# Kitchen spatial comparison — revised working study

September 14, 2026. Concept geometry, not a fit approval or cabinet plan.

**The strongest next candidate is a 36 × 60-inch chef island with a compact rear-corner sink allowance, three separate guest places with inward-turned ends, and a deeper left-of-range mise landing.** This is a direction to refine, not a decision to reduce the guest requirement. Four places and a longer island are explicitly compared in the drawing.

This pass produces two useful findings: angled guest ends can improve the stair-side counter turn, while a narrower, longer island makes the far-side produce area and helper/sink gap worse. It also exposes remaining problems in cleanup, appliance-open use and buffet circulation. No candidate has passed the complete occupied route.

## Left-of-range mise: improve depth before extending toward the stairs

The drawing tests a rectangular landing **24 inches along the range wall and 36 inches deep**, at Y 111.5–135.5. It does not extend its dining-side termination toward S1. Compared with the older nominal 27.5-inch depth, it projects an additional 8.5 inches into the kitchen and 5.5 inches beyond the range front.

The compact island ends at Y=98, before this landing begins. Consequently, the projection is beside the island's end, rather than narrowing the full working aisle opposite the board. The nearest landing-to-island corner separation is approximately 44.6 inches in this test. This is a local geometric check, not an occupied-turn approval.

The old counter is partly shaped, so its actual usable area and the gain from rebuilding it are not established. The full proposed rectangle cannot be counted exclusively for mise: hot-pan landing still has to be reserved using the real pans. This is a tangible enlargement hypothesis, not yet proof that the shortage is solved.

A 12-inch extension toward the stairs would bring the nominal termination to within about 29.4 inches in the Y direction of the S1 reference. That is not a stair clearance, but it illustrates why extending toward the stairs is a poor default. The present study explores depth instead.

## Angled ends have a spatial consequence

The straight three-seat reference spans X 72.5–162.5, with the helper edge at Y=148 and guest edge at Y=190. Each guest receives a **30-inch frontage allowance**, a 24-inch-wide occupied envelope and 26-inch outward projection. These are design assumptions, not new chair measurements or universal comfort requirements.

In the angled version, the two 30-inch end sections turn inward by 20 degrees. Guest and helper depth are measured perpendicular to their respective edges. The drawing uses continuous joined outlines; final supports, knee space, heights and join construction remain open.

**The angles trade space between two locations.** They pull the stair-side counter corners away from the mise landing, but bring the rear tips closer to the island. That is why they deserve a real test. They do not make floor area appear, and an occupied helper can still obstruct space that is clear between bare counters.

These are minimum distances between the illustrated polygons. They are not a verified 42-inch continuous route, nor do they include the missing stair outline. The 42-inch reference is the project's existing study target, not a code determination.

## Three versus four guests

Adding the fourth guest at the same 30-inch allowance grows the straight frontage from 90 to 120 inches toward the cleanup/M5 end. The angled ends reduce the projected span by only about **3.6 inches**, for either seating count. Their main value here is the shaped corner and conversation direction, not a large reduction in seating length.

The four-seat end-chair envelope comes within roughly **2.3 inches of the conditional M5 point when straight, or 7.1 inches with angled ends**. Those are point-to-envelope diagnostics, not surveyed wall clearances. They are strong warning signs that this placement cannot receive a four-seat circulation pass.

Three places avoid that particular near-contact, but still do not establish a clear route around the cleanup end. The fixed table's kitchen-side chair is shown with an occupied-size proxy, which further constrains the band behind the guest chairs. The normal table remains unmoved. Four comfortable places could require a different social location or configuration; this drawing does not prove that four is impossible throughout the room.

## Cleanup: the fixed face exposes a program problem

The shown straight cleanup envelope uses the existing 65-inch lower straight run, with a nominal 25-inch depth. The 30-inch sink module and 24-inch dishwasher allowance leave only **11 inches total** for other run length, fillers and landings. This is intentionally visible as an unresolved allocation, not a complete cleanup design.

The dishwasher is on the right when standing at and facing the sink. Moving the working face through the wall is not used to create room. Lengthening the run into the former diagonal area may be worth testing, but it must be checked against the new social-surface approach and actual wall termination. Cabinet depth and wall thickness are not established merely by the nominal envelope.

With the compact island, the closed family-side surface gap is 42.5 inches. A schematic 27-inch-open dishwasher reduces the local gap to 15.5 inches where the objects overlap. The oven-open test reduces the 48-inch range aisle to 21 inches before the chef's posture is considered. These use conservative illustrative door envelopes, not selected appliance specifications. Loading and oven use interrupt the respective positions; the drawing does not claim simultaneous use passes.

## Buffet and formal-dining movement

The buffet state uses the lower 18-inch band while guests remain at the high perch. The helper board is released in this state, and a plate-pickup person is drawn. This directly tests the requested independent buffet setup without clearing the guest surface.

The illustrated departure to Butler's pantry still enters the range aisle and crosses the chef's working position. It demonstrates the remaining conflict rather than solving it with a route arrow. On buffet occasions, an arrangement that relies on this aisle would need to tolerate pauses in serving or cooking; that operational compromise is not accepted on Jon's behalf.

The integrated porch transition remains a credible alternate buffet location, including an occasional temporary or drop-down surface. Its geometry is not invented in this plan. The secondary hall approach to formal dining remains a less-friendly fallback and does not automatically validate the kitchen route. Buffet use is at most 20 times annually, so it is not used to enlarge the daily chef island.

## Disposition and next physical checks

Advance the compact island / three angled places / deeper mise landing as the most useful **working combination**. Keep the four-seat requirement open until compared with other placements. Do not yet choose a sink product or cabinet dimensions.

The earlier continuous peninsula footprint can be switched on. It reaches the provisional S1 point, reinforcing the potential physical conflict at the range-side connection. That specific footprint should not be reused as a fit solution. Other attached geometries have not been ruled out categorically.

The next physical information that can change this comparison is the bottom-step and post-cap envelope, the actual outline of the left range landing, and the fixed cleanup wall's face/termination. The existing S1 ties and board measurements do not need repeating. These checks should be collected as one clearly identified batch before a clearance approval; no additional buffet or porch survey is required merely to discuss this study.

# Kitchen layout tradeoff pass

September 13, 2026 · Qualified first design pass · No layout approved

**Advance the continuous peninsula as the lead operational concept. Keep the range-facing worktable as the alternative. A shallower island offers a smaller improvement and still needs a convincing answer for linear mise space and traffic behind the chef. None yet demonstrates the complete occupied route.**

This pass draws candidate footprints, chef/helper positions, four separate guest places, dining-chair states, a 42-inch route target, and conditional appliance/door envelopes. It preserves the held range and the reconstructed normal table position. It does not substitute these design drawings for a field survey or the eventual reliable 3D model.

## What is being compared

There are two linked decisions: the chef's work arrangement, and the service/social boundary that has to accommodate four guests. They cannot be solved independently.

Keeping the approximately 79-inch older guest-front segment does not demonstrate four places even at 24 inches each: that would need 96 inches. This study uses **30 inches per guest**, making a **120-inch social edge**, with four separate chair envelopes. The 30-inch pitch is a design allowance for this brief, not a measured chair requirement or a universal comfort guarantee. Supports, knee space, height and the end condition at the range side still need design.

The first retention check therefore leaves both island alternatives with an unresolved guest-layer redesign. For a fair second comparison, the interactive plans deliberately give **all three arrangements the same proposed straight cleanup row and 120-inch range-connected social peninsula**. B and C consequently do not receive a claim of easier construction or less family-room impact. They are conditional combinations, not proof that a smaller island alone solves the brief.

The shared footprint is a test, not the only possible four-seat arrangement. A different social boundary could change the relative merits of B and C.

## A — continuous peninsula prep

**Best functional case:** remove the central island and put primary work along the kitchen-facing peninsula edge near the held range. The user faces the peninsula while preparing; a short turn toward the range replaces walking around an island.

The drawn sequence from the range connection toward the free end is:

**Prep water, 18-inch module → 36-inch board with trash below → separate 36-inch mise/landing area.** Small separating/end allowances remain within the 120-inch surface. The footprint does not establish a final sink installation, trash pullout or support structure.

The chef stands approximately at X=60, Y=127. The heat path goes toward the dining end of the held range. The helper has a separate 30-inch board/landing segment near the appliance end of the cleanup row, with the helper body centered approximately at X=160, Y=59. That person works well away from the chef-to-range movement. Refrigerator access is routed toward the appliance side, but the actual refrigerator doors/handles remain unlocated.

The absence of an island lets traffic turn into the center before reaching the dishwasher. It also removes the current island's physical protection: this layout must earn a protected chef position through circulation placement. The proposed route passes toward the free end and then appliance-side of the chef rather than directly through the heat movement. The open center is not automatically reserved for the chef.

**Unresolved:** family-room boundary and services; the range-side connection and loss of the existing pass-through; the dining/guest turn; the compact cleanup row's actual sink landings and loading posture. Using the sink near the mouth could encroach on the approach. The helper board may also need to become cleanup landing at other times. The concept demonstrates chef + helper use more convincingly than simultaneous chef + helper + active dishwasher loading.

## B — shallower island

**Test:** 33 × 57 inches, range edge retained at X=64, appliance edge retained at Y=38. Primary work stays at the family-room-facing edge, X=97.

Reducing depth from 51 to 33 removes **18 inches from the across-object separation**. It does not remove the island from the walking path to heat. The drawn path still goes around its dining end. The approximate field body-to-range value of 97 inches remains a separate observation; this pass does not convert the 18-inch reduction into a verified new travel distance.

The test fits an 18-inch prep-water module and a 30-inch board along the 57-inch front. Mise occupies a narrow rear strip, only about 10 inches deep within the approximate 28-inch reach from the chef edge. That is useful reachable surface, but it is not equivalent to A's separate 36-inch linear mise area. Choosing 30 or 36 inches of overall depth changes this reach/footprint balance without creating additional linear frontage.

The helper uses a separate 36-inch board on the peninsula, outside the immediate chef-to-range path. However, the gap from the island dining end to the peninsula front is **42 inches**. A schematic helper projecting 20 inches into it leaves **22 inches** on that section; it cannot simultaneously count as the project's through-route. The drawn through-route uses the family-room side instead.

That family-side gap is 60 inches to the older X=157 lower edge, or **73 inches to the proposed X=170 row**. This can attract traffic immediately behind the chef. With an assumed open dishwasher, the object-to-door gap becomes 46 inches; including the 20-inch chef working envelope leaves only 26 inches at overlapping sections. More empty floor has not independently solved protection or appliance access.

**Finding:** a credible incremental depth experiment, but the weak answer to the original shortage of contiguous mise space. Enlarging the 57-inch length toward dining consumes the 42-inch helper gap; enlarging it appliance-side spends the approximately 40-inch refrigerator clearance. Neither receives free credit.

## C — range-facing worktable

**Test:** 33 × 57 inches at X 78.5–111.5, Y 38–95. This places the range-facing edge 48 inches from the conditional X=30.5 range front. Keeping the old 33.5/34-inch aisle is not the selected test.

Primary prep occupies the range-facing edge. The chef turns between board and range without going around the full object. A 36-inch board and an 11-inch-long mise segment fit on the drawn worktable. Prep water is tested in an **18-inch module on the appliance-side range landing**, immediately across the aisle. That landing is historically described as 24 inches long and partly shaped; its usable footprint and sink feasibility are not established. This relocation must not erase necessary hot-pan landing. If water cannot work there, C must be reprogrammed; it cannot quietly borrow distant water and claim the same sequence.

Trash is below/beside the board and the helper has the peninsula board. The intended through-route uses the family-room side of the worktable, while the range aisle serves the chef. This protects the heat movement from the illustrated route, conditional on actual appliance-side access.

The **48-inch aisle falls to 21 inches with the assumed 27-inch oven projection**. The drawn 20-inch chef posture almost fills that remainder. A hot oven operation therefore requires the chef to step aside and stop using that prep position; the plan does not demonstrate simultaneous oven-open prep. The family-side gap is 58.5 inches closed, and **31.5 inches with the assumed dishwasher open**. That fails the project's 42-inch through-route target in that state.

**Finding:** the strongest alternative for short turning movements, with a real hot-aisle and water-placement tradeoff. It deserves a posture/appliance study, not acceptance based on the existing 34-inch gap.

## Occupied route and door findings

The normal table stays at the reconstructed TC/TF endpoints, drawn as a 48-inch-wide, approximately 66.6-inch-long capsule. Its relationship to the windows is preserved by leaving it fixed; an invented window wall is not added.

The French-door-end chair uses the confirmed 23.5-inch width and 19/36-inch tabletop-edge-to-back projections, with an assumed centered orientation. The opposite end and two side chairs use those same dimensions only as stress proxies. Their sizes and poses were not independently confirmed.

In this explicit arrangement, the **Y-direction separation** between the occupied guest backs and the schematic kitchen-side dining-chair back is approximately **33.6 inches**. Guest pullout alone reduces it to 23.6 inches. This is a projection between selected test envelopes, not a measured continuous width or full maneuvering result. It rules out claiming a straight 42-inch-wide route through that particular seating band. The end-around route is drawn as a 42-inch-wide target ribbon; it remains conditional at the guest/table turn and wall/door approach. Neither a centerline nor a locally clear mouth proves it fits.

The reported occupied-chair-back-to-French-door-center **37 inches** and pulled-out-back-to-closed-door/trim **26.5 inches** remain separate, valid field observations. They are not subtracted from one another or replaced by the drawing.

The optional French-door test draws two hypothetical 32-inch leaves, hinges starting 3 inches beyond M7, a flush wall continuation and 90-degree inward swings. **That assumed opening/plane/chair combination does not reconcile the recorded chair gaps. It is unsuitable for actual clearance conclusions.** The full sweeps are present to make the missing door geometry explicit, not to assert that the real doors collide. No widths, hinge positions, setbacks or handing were measured in this pass. Refrigerator and wall-oven positions also remain topology-only, so full appliance-wall door/access validation is not complete.

## Comparison and decision

| Criterion | A: peninsula prep | B: shallower island | C: range-facing worktable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary sequence near heat | Most complete drawn sequence; separate board and mise | Reduced depth; still walks around object; narrow rear mise strip | Short turn; water depends on range landing |
| Chef protection | Relies on keeping route away from peninsula working position | Traffic remains behind chef in enlarged aisle | Illustrated traffic stays outside range aisle |
| Useful helper | Separate board on cleanup row, remote from heat path | Peninsula board; occupied gap cannot be through-route | Same peninsula board and gap limitation |
| Four separate guest places | Explicitly drawn | Same required social upgrade | Same required social upgrade |
| Table/window protection | Fixed table | Fixed table | Fixed table |
| Continuous occupied route | Not demonstrated at dining/door turn | Same turn plus chef/door conflicts | Same turn plus dishwasher-open conflict |
| Architectural/service cost | Removes island; changes cleanup and range/dining connection | Same common changes plus island rebuild | Same common changes plus water relocation/worktable shift |

**Design priority, not a build ranking:** develop A first because it most directly addresses the full prep sequence. Preserve C as the comparison if pivot cooking proves preferable. B needs a materially better work-surface program before an 18-inch depth saving alone justifies it.

No new tape measurement is requested now. The next design iteration can work on A's guest/table turn and cleanup loading position without moving the table or counting occupied floor as clear passage. Actual wall/door/appliance envelopes and services are the specific information required before any candidate can be approved; this pass does not declare the complete comparison finalized.
